pendulum

How it works

Place and launch. In the lab view, drag the ball anywhere. Lift your finger without moving to release it at rest; swipe and let go to give it the velocity of your swipe. Space pauses, R resets to the last release, V switches view.

Three ropes

  • Rigid rod: length fixed. It can pull (tension) or push (compression) on the ball.
  • String: it can only pull. When the tension would become negative the string goes slack and the ball flies as a projectile until the string snaps tight again. That jerk removes the radial velocity, and its kinetic energy goes into the dissipated bar.
  • Elastic: a rope that stretches like a spring, T = k(r − L), and goes slack when shorter than L.

Forces and the third law

On the ball act the weight W (Earth on ball), the rope force T (rope on ball) and the resistance D (air on ball). Each has a partner of equal size and opposite direction acting on another body: the ball pulls the rope, the support pulls the rope, the rope pulls the support, the ball pulls the Earth. Partners carry the same number and colour; the partner arrow has a hollow head. The rope is massless, so the two forces on it always cancel.

Split along rope ∥ and ⊥ decomposes the forces along the rope and perpendicular to it. The ∥ parts give the centripetal force m v²/L, the ⊥ parts change the speed.

Ride on the ball

In the ball view the camera follows the ball, which is at rest in this frame. To keep Newton's second law working you must add the apparent force −m·a, where a is the acceleration of the frame. Its rope-wise part is the centrifugal force, its other part opposes the tangential acceleration. In this frame W + T + D − m·a = 0.

The liquid

The liquid surface settles perpendicular to the effective gravity g − a. Without drag and friction, g − a equals T/m along the rope, so the surface always stays perpendicular to the rope, even at the top of the swing. Add drag and the surface tilts. With a slack string the ball is in free fall: g − a ≈ 0 and the liquid floats.

Energy

K kinetic, Ug gravitational (zero at the lowest point of the circle), Uel elastic, Q dissipated by drag, friction and string jerks. The total bar stays constant: what leaves the mechanical bars shows up in Q.

Pivot friction

A rigid rod rubbing on its pivot feels a braking torque proportional to ω. The rod passes it to the ball as a force across the rope, so the rope force is no longer along the rope. A string cannot carry a torque, so this option works only with the rod.

Force polygon

The forces on ball A drawn tip to tail. In the lab view the dashed side that closes the shape is ΣF = m·a. In the ball view −m·a is added and the polygon closes: the forces sum to zero.

Two pendulums

Pendulum B hangs beside A. Independent: compare lengths, masses or amplitudes (isochronism). Coupled by a spring: energy flows from one to the other with the beat period shown in the Graphs tab. The two spring forces are a third-law pair (number 4).

Driven support

The support of A shakes as A·sin(2πft). Near f₀ the amplitude grows (resonance); strong drives give chaotic motion. Fast vertical shaking can keep the rod upside down (Kapitza pendulum). The energy bar W is the work done by the support; Σ − W stays constant.

Phone on the ball

A phone strapped to ball A reads what phyphox would show: the accelerometer measures a − g ("with g", reads +9.81 at rest) or a ("without g") along its own axes, y toward the support and x along the swing; the gyroscope reads ω. Pick these quantities in the Graphs tab to compare with a real phone swung on a string.

Liquid models

Simple surface: a damped surface that tilts toward perpendicular to g − a. 2D particles: about two hundred particles that push each other apart, simulated inside the ball. Slower on old phones.
